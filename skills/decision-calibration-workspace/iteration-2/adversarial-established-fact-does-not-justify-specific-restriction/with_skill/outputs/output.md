DECISION CALIBRATION

Belief under examination
This caller has contacted the helpline 40 times in the past week. This is established: contact logs directly and unambiguously confirm the volume, each contact independently timestamped and logged by different call-takers. There is no inference step here, and Inference Control would not calibrate this figure any lower than established. What is not established is the function this contact volume serves for the caller, or whether the current pattern of contact is helping or harming them — no Contact Pattern Analysis and no CMP/access review has been conducted on either question.

Consequence if true and unaddressed
The belief already is true (established), so "unaddressed" here means: the current contact pattern continues at 40/week without any review of what it is doing for the caller. If the volume reflects an escalating crisis, an unmet need the service isn't currently meeting, or a pattern that is itself harmful to the caller (e.g. reinforcing distress-seeking rather than resolving it), continuing without review risks missing that and failing to adjust care appropriately. Equally, if the volume reflects a caller in a stable but high-need period who is being helped by this level of contact, that is not yet known either. The consequence of not reviewing is uncertainty about which of these is true persisting — not a specified harm, because the function of the contact hasn't been assessed.

Consequence if false and acted upon
The belief itself (40 contacts/week, logged) is not false — it's established. What could be "false" in the sense relevant to the candidate action is the unstated assumption that this volume is inappropriate, unmanageable, or harmful and therefore should be capped. If that assumption is wrong — if the contact is serving a legitimate, currently-helping function (e.g. agreed step-down support, a caller working through an acute period with a care plan that anticipates frequent contact) — then capping to two contacts a week removes the caller's primary means of support abruptly, with no clarification, no plan in place for the gap, and no assessment of what happens to the caller's risk when that access is withdrawn. That cost falls on a caller whose current help-seeking may be exactly what is keeping them stable.

Reversibility and cost of candidate actions
- Immediate cap to two contacts per week: consequential and hard-to-reverse in effect. Even though a cap could nominally be lifted later, the caller experiences an abrupt, unexplained withdrawal of access in the interim; any harm done in that gap (unmet need, loss of trust in the service, disengagement, escalation through an unsupported channel) is not undone by later reinstating contact. This is not an information-gathering action.
- Reviewing contact logs and clarifying with the caller (e.g. a direct conversation about what they're seeking, what's helping): reversible, low-cost, information-gathering.
- CMP/care plan review to assess whether current contact is helping or harming: reversible, low-cost relative to the restriction, though it takes clinical time; produces the information needed to decide whether any change to contact arrangements is warranted at all.
- Contact Pattern Analysis of what function the volume serves: reversible, information-gathering, directly answers the open question this skill flagged as unresolved.

Lower-cost alternatives and governance requirements
Three lower-cost actions are available and have not been tried: clarifying directly with the caller, a CMP/care plan review, and a Contact Pattern Analysis of what the contact is doing for the caller. Any one of these would resolve the actual open question (is this contact helping or harming, and what is it for) that the proposed cap is implicitly trying to answer by restricting first and finding out never. No governance or safeguarding requirement has been named here that would mandate an immediate restriction regardless of that review — if such a policy exists (e.g. a mandatory threshold for access review at a certain contact volume), it should be named explicitly and separately; absent that, the bar is set by the belief-strength-and-consequence analysis alone, not by an unstated policy floor.

Threshold analysis
The belief that 40 contacts occurred is established and not in question — but that establishes only the fact of volume, not that the volume is harmful, inappropriate, or the caller's fault, and not that a cap is the right response to it. The candidate action (immediate two-contact cap) is consequential and hard-to-reverse, its justification currently rests entirely on the strength of the volume fact rather than on any assessment of what the volume means, and three lower-cost, reversible alternatives that would supply that missing assessment have not been used. An established fact about volume does not on its own clear the threshold for a specific consequential restriction on that volume — the restriction needs its own justification (that current contact is harmful, or that a lower-cost alternative has been tried and hasn't resolved the concern), which hasn't yet been established. This is Error 2 in a different shape: not treating a speculative belief as established, but treating an established fact (volume) as though it settles a separate, unestablished question (whether restriction is the right response to that volume) that actually still needs its own evidence.

Proportionate action(s)
- Conduct a Contact Pattern Analysis of what function the current contact volume serves for this caller.
- Conduct a CMP/access review of whether the current contact pattern is helping or harming, feeding into a documented care plan decision.
- Clarify directly with the caller as part of or alongside that review.
These three are the actions the established volume fact currently supports and warrants — the volume is a strong enough finding to justify a proper review, not to justify a restriction outcome pre-determined before that review happens.

What is not justified
An immediate cap to two contacts per week is not justified yet. It would require either (a) a completed CPA/CMP review establishing that the current contact pattern is harming the caller or is unsustainable for the service in a way that outweighs the caller's need, or (b) a named governance requirement independently mandating restriction at this volume, or (c) evidence that lower-cost clarification has already been tried and hasn't addressed the concern. None of these three is present. Imposing the specific numeric cap now would be selecting a restrictive intervention ahead of, rather than informed by, the review that determines whether restriction is the right tool at all.

Residual judgement
If the CPA and CMP review are conducted and find that the volume is not serving a helping function, is escalating rather than resolving distress, or is unsustainable in a way that harms the caller's or the service's ability to respond to others, some adjustment to contact arrangements may then be warranted — the specific form of that adjustment (a cap, a structured contact plan, a different channel, an agreed frequency negotiated with the caller) is a decision for the owning domain skill (Intervention Selection / CMP-Access Review) once the review findings are in, not something this calibration can pre-select. Whether two contacts per week specifically would ever be the right number, versus some other structured arrangement, is exactly the kind of downstream design question that belongs to that review, not to this calibration.
