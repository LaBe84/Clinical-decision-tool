DECISION CALIBRATION

Belief under examination
Four independently documented contacts show a caller's presentation reliably deteriorates following contact with a specific family member. Inference Control has calibrated this as a well-supported association — established by pattern, not by causal mechanism. It is not established that the contact *causes* the deterioration, only that the two are reliably linked across independent observations. No one is proposing to cut off family contact, so restriction of contact is off the table as a framing from the outset.

Consequence if true and unaddressed
If the association is real and nothing is done, the caller will likely continue to experience predictable spikes in distress or risk around contact with this family member, and the service will keep responding to those spikes reactively (each one arriving as a fresh crisis) rather than anticipating them. The specific, non-catastrophised cost of inaction is missed opportunity: a known, recurring risk window goes unprepared-for, repeatedly, when it could instead be planned around. It is not established that unaddressed contact leads to an irreversible or escalating outcome — that would overstate what four documented instances show.

Consequence if false and acted upon
If the association turns out to be coincidental or confounded (e.g., both driven by an unrelated cyclical stressor, or by something the family member represents rather than the contact itself), low-cost responses (see below) cost little: some clinician time, a slightly more structured intake around those contacts, possibly a conversation with the caller that surfaces a wrong hypothesis and can be corrected. Higher-cost responses — anything that treats the family member as a confirmed cause and structures care around excluding or restricting that relationship — would cost more if wrong: damage to the caller's relationship with a family member who may not be the actual driver, damage to trust in the service if the caller experiences the response as the service deciding who they should see, and a foreclosed relationship that's hard to rebuild if the inference was mistaken.

Reversibility and cost of candidate actions
- Flagging the pattern in the caller's record for responders to see at future contact — reversible, low-cost, information-preserving.
- Briefly noting the pattern to the caller and asking what they notice about those contacts — reversible, low-cost, information-gathering; also treats the caller as the authority on their own experience rather than acting on their behalf.
- Building a light anticipatory check-in (a scheduled or offered contact point) around times this family member is expected to be in contact — reversible, low-cost, precautionary.
- Incorporating the pattern into safety planning as one factor among others (a heads-up trigger, not a directive) — reversible, moderate cost, and belongs to Safety Planning's domain to design, not this skill's to prescribe.
- Raising it with the caller's broader care team as a documented observation for clinical judgement — reversible, moderate cost.
- Treating the family member's contact as a confirmed causal trigger and structuring an intervention around limiting, monitoring, or gatekeeping that contact — consequential, hard to reverse relationally, and explicitly not what anyone is proposing here; also not warranted by "well-supported association" alone, since that stops short of established causation.

Threshold analysis
A well-supported association clears the threshold for the reversible, information-gathering, and precautionary actions listed above: the consequence of inaction (a recurring, unprepared-for risk window) is real and specific enough to justify low-cost anticipatory steps, and the cost if the association turns out to be non-causal is small and correctable. It does not clear the threshold for treating the family member's contact as a confirmed trigger and building a restrictive or gatekeeping response around it — that requires closer to established causal confidence, not a well-supported association, precisely because the cost if wrong (a damaged relationship, a foreclosed contact, a caller who experiences the service as overriding their choices) is high and hard to reverse. This matches the stated premise: no one is proposing restriction, and the calibration agrees that nothing here would justify it.

Proportionate action(s)
- Flag the pattern in the record so future responders have it as context, not as a directive.
- Ask the caller directly what they notice about those contacts, and hold their account alongside the documented pattern rather than substituting for it.
- Offer or schedule a light anticipatory check-in around expected contact windows.
- Bring the pattern to the care team / relevant domain-decision process (Safety Planning, CRG Triage, whichever owns the caller's ongoing plan) as a documented factor for their judgement — not as this skill's prescription of what the plan should say.
More than one of these clears the threshold; none of them requires choosing a single "the" response, and they are not mutually exclusive — several can run concurrently.

What is not justified
Any response that treats the family member's contact as an established, confirmed cause of deterioration and builds monitoring, gatekeeping, or restriction of that contact around it. That response requires established-level causal confidence; what's actually in hand is a well-supported association, explicitly not a causal mechanism. Using the strength of "four independently documented contacts" to justify a restrictive intervention would borrow weight the finding hasn't earned — and would also contradict the stated premise that restriction isn't on the table.

Residual judgement
Which of the proportionate, lower-cost actions to lead with — flagging in the record, asking the caller directly, scheduling a check-in, or routing to the care team — is a judgement call that depends on the caller's current relationship with the service, how they're likely to respond to the pattern being named to them, and operational capacity for anticipatory check-ins. This calibration does not resolve that choice; it establishes that several of these options are proportionate and leaves the specific selection to the clinician or the owning domain skill (Safety Planning / CRG Triage), consistent with this skill's boundary against selecting the specific intervention itself.
