DECISION CALIBRATION

Belief under examination
The caller's contact pattern might reflect reassurance-seeking that could reinforce distress. Inference Control has calibrated this as consistent with/plausible — CPA has flagged the underlying mechanism (reassurance-seeking reinforcing distress) as a hypothesis, not confirmed. This is not established or well-supported; it is a plausible reading of the pattern, one of potentially several.

Consequence if true and unaddressed
If the pattern really is reassurance-seeking that reinforces distress, continued unrestricted contact may sustain or deepen a cycle where the caller relies on repeated contact rather than developing other coping responses, and the underlying distress does not improve. This is a real but graded and slow-moving harm — it plays out over repeated contacts, not in a single call — and it is itself uncertain: the same contact pattern could instead reflect a genuine and appropriate ongoing need for support, in which case "unaddressed" is not actually harmful. The specific consequence of inaction, then, is a possible failure to interrupt a reinforcement cycle, not a certain one, and not an acute safety event.

Consequence if false and acted upon
If the reassurance-seeking hypothesis is wrong and access is restricted anyway, the caller loses the option to reach the helpline when they may urgently need it, at exactly the times a fixed weekly cap would prevent. This risks: reduced help-seeking generally, mistrust of the service, a sense of being penalised for contacting a crisis line (which is the opposite of the line's function), and — if the caller's actual state involves real risk that the contact pattern was appropriately tracking — a removed safety net at a moment it was needed. These costs are severe, not marginal, and they land on the person the intervention is meant to help.

Reversibility and cost of candidate actions
- Immediately restricting access to a fixed number of contacts per week: consequential and hard to reverse in practice. Even if formally revocable, it changes the caller's relationship with the service, may not be trusted to be temporary once experienced, and carries real safety cost if wrong. This is a high-cost, high-consequence action.
- Alternative: raising the pattern with the caller directly, exploring what the repeated contact is meeting for them, and naming reassurance-seeking as a possibility collaboratively: reversible, information-gathering, low-cost.
- Alternative: bringing the pattern to clinical/team review or supervision to track it over further contacts before deciding anything: reversible, low-cost, does not touch the caller's access at all.
- Alternative: agreeing a caller-involved plan (e.g., a self-chosen contact strategy, alternative coping supports offered alongside continued full access): low-to-moderate cost, more reversible than a unilateral cap, and does not remove access.

Threshold analysis
Restricting access outright requires something close to an established or well-supported finding that (a) the pattern is reassurance-seeking and (b) that reassurance-seeking is reinforcing distress in a way that outweighs the caller's need for the line — plus confidence that a hard cap is a safe response to that finding. Here the belief is speculative-to-plausible only, explicitly flagged by CPA as a hypothesis, and the consequence of inaction is graded and uncertain rather than acute. That does not clear the bar for a consequential, hard-to-reverse, access-restricting action. By contrast, the low-cost/reversible options (direct conversation, team review, collaborative planning) require far less belief strength to justify — a plausible hypothesis with real stakes if true is enough to warrant looking closer or naming the pattern to the caller. Those options clear their (much lower) threshold easily.

Proportionate action(s)
- Raise the observed pattern with the caller directly and explore it collaboratively, rather than acting on it unilaterally.
- Escalate the pattern to clinical/team review or supervision for tracking and a second opinion before any access-level decision is made.
- If a change is warranted, pursue it as a caller-involved plan (offering alternative supports, discussing what would help) rather than an imposed cap — and only once the hypothesis has been tested further, not on the current speculative footing.
Any of these would be proportionate; none of them requires restricting access now.

What is not justified
Immediately imposing a fixed weekly contact restriction. This action requires an established or well-supported causal finding to justify its cost and irreversibility, and what exists here is a flagged hypothesis at consistent-with/plausible strength. Acting on it as though it were confirmed borrows the weight of a strong claim for a claim that has not earned it (Error 2) — treating "the mechanism is plausible" as though it meant "the mechanism is established and restriction is safe."

Residual judgement
If, after direct conversation and team review, the pattern persists and the reassurance-seeking reading strengthens (e.g., corroborated by the caller's own account or repeated clinical assessment), the calibration could shift toward considering some access arrangement — but that is a decision for CMP/Access Review to make on the strength of evidence at that later point, not a foreclosure decided now. Which of the low-cost options to pursue first (direct conversation vs. team review) is itself a matter of clinical judgement and caller context that this skill does not resolve — either is defensible as a starting point, and they are not mutually exclusive.
