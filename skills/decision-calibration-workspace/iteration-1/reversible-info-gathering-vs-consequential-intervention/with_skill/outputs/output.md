DECISION CALIBRATION

Belief under examination
The caller's living situation may have changed recently in a way that affects safety planning. Two contact notes give conflicting accounts of who the caller lives with (a contradictory-evidence case per Evidence-Gap Detection). Inference Control has not yet resolved which account is more likely correct. Calibrated strength: consistent with / plausible — there is a real, evidenced discrepancy, but no basis yet to say which account is accurate, or that the living situation has actually changed versus one note being mistaken, outdated, or referring to a different point in time.

Consequence if true and unaddressed
If the living situation has genuinely changed and this goes unaddressed, the safety plan may rely on a household composition, support person, or means-restriction arrangement that no longer holds — e.g. an assumed protective co-resident who is no longer present, or an assumed absence of a risk factor (access to means, a difficult household relationship) that is now present. This is a real and specific risk to the adequacy of the safety plan, not a diffuse one. It is also uncertain in a second sense: even if the situation has changed, it may not affect the specific safety-plan elements that matter (e.g. a change of housemate that doesn't touch access to means or support availability). The consequence is contingent on both "the change is real" and "the change matters to safety planning" — both currently unresolved.

Consequence if false and acted upon
For action (a), asking the caller directly at next contact: if the concern is unfounded (e.g. a data-entry inconsistency, or both notes were accurate at different times and nothing has changed), the cost is negligible — a short clarifying question that a caller can answer or decline to answer with no lasting effect on the relationship.
For action (b), involving a third party to verify the caller's home situation without the caller's knowledge: if the concern is unfounded, the cost is substantial and not easily undone — it involves contacting someone in the caller's life about their circumstances behind their back, which can damage the caller's trust in the service, alter their relationship with that third party, and — if the caller later learns of it — actively deter future disclosure or engagement. This cost exists independent of whether the belief turns out true or false; it is a cost of the method, not just of being wrong.

Reversibility and cost of candidate actions
(a) Ask the caller directly at next contact — reversible, information-gathering. It changes nothing about the caller's world; it only seeks clarification through the caller's own voice, and can be dropped, rephrased, or deferred without consequence.
(b) Proactively involve a named third party to verify the caller's home situation without the caller's knowledge — consequential, largely irreversible. It changes the caller's world (someone now knows the service is asking questions about them) regardless of what is found, breaches the caller's expectation of confidentiality/autonomy in how their situation is investigated, and cannot be fully undone once the contact is made — the third party's knowledge that this happened does not go away even if the finding turns out to support no concern at all.

Threshold analysis
A consistent-with/plausible belief, with a real but not yet catastrophic consequence-if-true and a consequence-if-false that is negligible, comfortably clears the bar for a reversible, low-cost, information-gathering action. Action (a) requires only that there be a genuine reason to ask — which the contradictory notes supply — not that the discrepancy be resolved or the risk be confirmed.
Action (b) sits at the opposite end of the cost/reversibility axis. Undisclosed third-party verification is the kind of action that would need something closer to an established or well-supported finding of active concealment or acute danger to justify its cost — a documented contradiction between two notes, with no resolution attempted yet through the caller themselves, does not meet that bar. Using it here would be borrowing the weight of a strong claim (active risk requiring covert verification) for a plausible-level administrative discrepancy — the mirror-image error the skill flags. It also skips a lower-cost step (asking the caller) that has not yet been tried and would very plausibly resolve the question on its own.

Proportionate action(s)
- Ask the caller directly about their current living situation at the next contact (action a) — clearly justified now, independent of how the discrepancy resolves.
- If the caller's answer is itself evasive, inconsistent with both prior notes, or the caller declines to answer in a way that itself raises new concern, that new information can be fed back into Inference Control to reassess belief strength — which may then justify escalated enquiry through the normal domain-skill pathway (not this skill's job to specify which).
- Document the contradiction and the plan to clarify it, so it isn't lost if the caller isn't reached at the next contact and a different worker picks up the case.

What is not justified
- Involving a third party to verify the caller's home situation without the caller's knowledge (action b), on the strength of a contradictory-evidence finding alone. This would require a materially stronger, more specific belief (e.g. established indication of active danger to the caller that direct questioning cannot safely or reliably resolve) — not merely two conflicting contact notes — and even then would be a decision for the owning domain skill (e.g. Intervention Selection or CMP/Access Review), not one this skill or a single worker should default into as a first move.
- Treating "we can't yet tell which note is right" as a reason to do nothing until certainty arrives — the low-cost option (asking the caller) is available now and does not require resolving the contradiction first.

Residual judgement
If the caller cannot be reached at the next scheduled contact, or gives an answer that is itself ambiguous, there is genuine room for judgement about what happens next — for instance, whether a shorter follow-up interval is warranted, or whether the contradiction alone (still unresolved) is enough to flag the case for review by someone with fuller context. That judgement belongs to Inference Control (has the belief strength changed) and the relevant domain skill (what enquiry or safety-planning step follows) once the direct-question step has been tried — this skill does not resolve it in advance, and covert third-party verification should not be reached for as a default fallback simply because the direct question didn't fully settle the matter.
