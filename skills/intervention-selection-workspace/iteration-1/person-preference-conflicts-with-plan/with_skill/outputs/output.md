INTERVENTION SELECTION

Target
Not fully established as a single item, and that has to be said plainly
rather than papered over. Two candidate targets are on the table and they
are not the same thing:
(a) reduce the distress and reluctance caused by the current escalation
    step (re-explaining her situation to a new person each time she calls
    the crisis line), and
(b) determine whether her stated preference (contacting one specific
    trusted person instead) is itself a safe and adequate substitute for
    the crisis-line step at the moments the plan calls for escalation.

(a) is supported directly by what she has said. (b) is not yet established
— we don't yet know who this person is, whether they've agreed to and can
sustain this role, whether they can act if she is in danger, or whether the
plan's original reasons for specifying the crisis line (e.g. availability,
trained response, out-of-hours cover) still apply.

Established basis
- She has an existing safety plan naming the crisis line as the primary
  escalation step.
- She has directly and specifically stated a preference against that step,
  with a clear, credible reason (repeated disclosure to unfamiliar people
  is itself distressing) — this is not vague dissatisfaction, it's a named
  mechanism of harm in the current plan.
- She has proposed a specific alternative (one particular trusted person),
  not a vague wish to avoid escalation altogether.
- No information here indicates immediate danger; this is a plan-design
  conversation, not a crisis-in-progress.
- No information yet establishes the trusted person's suitability,
  availability, consent, or capability to respond appropriately if
  escalation is needed. That gap is real and must not be filled by
  assumption.

Per the boundary rules, her preference must not be refused automatically
because it conflicts with the existing plan — it is clinically meaningful
information that may justify modifying the plan. Equally, it must not be
adopted automatically just because she prefers it; a safety plan's
escalation step exists to be reliable under pressure, and swapping it
requires the same scrutiny as installing it in the first place.

Selected response
Destination 2 (Modify current response) is likely the right eventual
destination, but it cannot be finalized in this conversation because a
piece of destination 6 (seek additional information) sits in front of it:
before the plan is changed, the substitute step needs to be checked for
adequacy — not to gatekeep her preference, but because an escalation step
that fails when actually needed is a worse outcome for her than the
distress the current step causes.

So, concretely, two things happen together, not sequentially with her
distress ignored in the meantime:
1. Acknowledge and validate the preference now, and treat it as the trigger
   to revisit the plan — this is not deferred or minimized.
2. Before finalizing the substitution, establish with her: does the
   trusted person know they may be called in a crisis and have they agreed
   to that role; can they be reached at the times/circumstances the plan
   anticipates; what should happen if that person is unavailable or if the
   situation is beyond what a non-professional contact can safely handle.
   This is information-gathering done collaboratively with her, not a
   unilateral withholding of her preference.

If those questions check out, the plan is modified to reflect her
preference (possibly with the trusted person as a first step and the
crisis line retained as a named fallback, rather than removed outright) —
that specific shape is a decision for the safety-planning process itself,
not this skill. If they don't check out, the conversation is with her about
what would make an alternative viable, not a reversion to the old plan
without explanation.

Mechanism
We are naming the distress mechanism and pausing to verify the substitute
step's adequacy (X) because we expect this to influence whether the
revised plan is both trusted and reliably followed by her under pressure
(Y), through removing the specific friction that currently makes her less
likely to use the plan at all (repeated disclosure) while confirming the
new step doesn't silently remove protection at the moment it's needed (Z).

Proportionality
This is proportionate to what's established: a stated, specific,
reasoned preference about one step of an existing plan. It doesn't require
escalation, doesn't require overriding her, and doesn't require accepting
the substitution untested. Treating this as a full re-triage or elevating
concern because she's pushing back on the plan would be disproportionate —
there's no indication here of increased risk, only a plan-design friction
point she's articulated clearly.

Risks / unintended effects
- If the crisis line is simply dropped without checking the substitute:
  risk of a single point of failure if the trusted person is unavailable,
  unequipped, or unwilling in the actual moment of crisis — a competing
  safety problem introduced in the name of respecting preference.
- If the preference is refused outright: risk of reduced trust in the plan
  and reduced likelihood she uses any part of it, including the parts that
  currently work — undermining the plan's overall function rather than
  protecting it.
- If this is treated as requiring formal reassessment of risk or triage:
  disproportionate response to a plan-design preference, and a misuse of
  this skill's boundary (this is not a re-formulation of risk).
- Dependency risk on a single named individual should be named to her
  directly as part of the conversation, not silently accepted or silently
  overridden.

Ownership
This is Safety Planning's territory for the actual redesign of the
escalation step — this skill identifies that modification is indicated and
what needs verifying first, but does not draft the new plan content. The
crisis-line worker/case holder should: validate her stated preference in
the conversation, gather the adequacy information with her (not about her),
and route the confirmed change to whoever owns her safety plan for formal
update.

Review
- We will know this is working if the revised plan is one she reports
  she would actually use, and the substitute contact's suitability has
  been explicitly confirmed (not assumed) by her and, where practicable,
  by the trusted person themselves.
- We will reconsider it if the trusted person turns out to be unavailable,
  unwilling, or unable to respond appropriately when tested against the
  scenarios the plan anticipates, or if she reports the new step is also
  not being used.
- We will review by the next scheduled safety-plan review, or sooner if
  either of the above conditions arises.

Residual uncertainty
- Who the trusted person is, whether they've agreed to this role, and
  whether they are capable of an appropriate response in the specific
  situations the plan covers — none of this is established yet.
- Whether the original reasons the crisis line was specified (e.g.
  professional response capacity, availability outside the trusted
  person's reach) still apply and need to be preserved as a fallback.
- Whether her reluctance is specific to the crisis line only, or reflects
  a broader difficulty with the escalation step generally that a different
  named contact would also eventually run into.
