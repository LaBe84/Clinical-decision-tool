INTERVENTION SELECTION

Target
Not yet established. The material presented is staff-reported exhaustion in relation to a particular caller. That is a staff-experience observation, not a stated clinical or service-delivery target. No formulation currently identifies what would need to change about this caller's contact pattern, risk state, or care need — because no Contact Pattern Analysis has been done.

Established basis
What is established: staff report feeling exhausted by contact with this caller.
What is not established: any contact-pattern finding (frequency, escalation-linkage, reinforcement pattern), any documented effect on other callers or on service capacity, or any clinical basis for treating this caller's contact as the target of an intervention. Staff fatigue is a real and legitimate operational signal, but on its own it is evidence of staff experience, not evidence of service-delivery impact or clinical need.

Selected response
Destination 6 — seek additional information before selecting an intervention. Access should not be restricted on this basis as presented. This is not a judgment that restriction will never be warranted; it is that the specific evidence required to select it — a completed Contact Pattern Analysis and documented service-delivery impact — has not yet been produced.

If access restriction is to be considered once that evidence exists, it becomes CMP/Access Review's decision, not this skill's, and would need its own explicit purpose, expected effect, and review mechanism at that point.

Mechanism
Not applicable — no intervention is being proposed. Completing "we are doing X because we expect it to influence Y through Z" is not possible here: X (access restriction) has no established Y (there is no stated target) and therefore no credible Z (mechanism cannot be specified against an unstated target).

Proportionality
Not assessable yet. Proportionality is judged against an established need; here the need itself has not been established, only a staff-reported experience. Proposing a specific intensity of response (including restriction) before the target exists would be reverse-justification — picking the action first and back-filling the rationale.

Risks / unintended effects
Naming these prospectively, in case this is revisited once evidence is gathered: an access restriction whose basis is staff fatigue rather than an established clinical or service-delivery need risks (a) being applied without a defined review mechanism, since there is no evidenced target to review against; (b) obscuring whether the actual issue is workload distribution, training, or debriefing support rather than this caller's contact itself; (c) being applied inconsistently compared with other high-contact callers if the rationale is not evidence-based; and (d) leaving the underlying operational problem — if one exists — unaddressed because the response was aimed at the caller rather than at whatever the evidence, once gathered, actually shows.

Ownership
Not applicable at this stage — no intervention is assigned.

Review
Not applicable — no intervention selected. What would change this: a completed Contact Pattern Analysis, and separately, documentation of actual service-delivery impact (effect on other callers, on staffing capacity, or on service function). Route back to Clinical Workflow Router to establish which upstream analysis is needed before this skill is re-run.

Residual uncertainty
Whether this caller's contact pattern is in fact escalation-linked, reinforcing, or otherwise clinically significant is unknown — that is precisely what has not yet been analysed. Whether staff exhaustion reflects a genuine, addressable service-capacity problem (which may warrant an operational response quite different from restricting this caller's access) is also unknown. Both should be named as open rather than resolved by the presence of the exhaustion report alone.

Note on framing
Staff workload and sustainable service delivery are legitimate operational considerations and are not dismissed here. But they are named explicitly as operational, not clinical, and they are not — by themselves — sufficient basis for changing an individual caller's access. The relevant question going forward is evidentiary: whether a documented service-delivery impact and an established contact-pattern basis exist to support restriction, not whether restricting access would relieve staff discomfort in the meantime.
