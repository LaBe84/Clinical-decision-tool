# Safety Plan Review

**Caller:** 29yo, late-night intoxicated impulsive overdose risk following breakups, prescribed medication kept at home.

## Verdict: Mostly usable — strong, person-specific plan with two real gaps, not cosmetic ones

This is a genuinely good plan by the standard that matters: almost every line survives the "would this actually work for *this* person, drunk, at 2am, ashamed, alone" test rather than the "does this sound responsible on paper" test. It should not be rewritten from scratch. It needs two additions, not a redesign.

---

### What's working (and why it clears the bar, not just reads well)

**Early warning sign — "3+ drinks and start texting my ex."**
This is a real behavioural marker, not a feeling or a vague mood description. It's externally observable, tied to the caller's own documented pattern (breakup-triggered, alcohol-involved), and — critically — it fires *before* the point of maximum danger rather than only once she's already mid-crisis. Passes the "early enough to act" test.

**"CALL FIRST" saved at the top of contacts.**
This is designed around impairment, not around a sober person's competence. It requires no memory of a plan document, no searching, no decision-making beyond "open contacts, tap the top one." That's exactly the right level of friction-reduction for an intoxicated state where recall and executive function are degraded.

**Freezer method for the phone.**
This is the standout element — a concrete, idiosyncratic, self-devised friction technique aimed directly at the impulsive/intoxicated texting-the-ex behaviour that the plan itself names as the trigger. It doesn't ask her to "resist the urge" through willpower; it inserts a physical delay that degrades her ability to act on the urge, which is the correct mechanism for an impulsive-intoxicated presentation. This is person-specific in a way generic plans never are.

**Excluding a friend from the escalation step, with reasoning given.**
"Friends don't always pick up at 2am, the line does" is not a throwaway line — it's the plan correctly distinguishing a protective factor that exists in her life from one that's actually available at the relevant hour. This is exactly the discipline the skill calls for: not listing a person as a resource just because they exist.

**Sister holding the medication bottle during flagged risk weeks.**
Good use of means restriction, framed as a practical arrangement rather than a test of intent, and it depends on stated willingness (sister has agreed) rather than an assumed protective factor.

---

### Gaps that need to be closed before this is complete

**1. Means restriction only covers weeks she predicts in advance — the risk pattern is impulsive, not predictable.**
The medication-removal step depends entirely on a Sunday-night forecast of "this week is a risk week." But the crisis pattern named for this plan is *impulsive* overdose risk after breakups — the kind of thing that can be triggered by an unexpected contact from an ex, a bad night, a fight, none of which reliably show up on a Sunday forecast. As written, if the crisis state occurs during a week she didn't flag, the medication is sitting at home with no plan covering it. This needs one of:
- a same-day/short-notice option ("if it's not a flagged week but I know tonight is bad, I text my sister and she collects it that evening"), or
- an explicit acknowledgment that the freezer/phone step and crisis-line call are the only safeguards during unflagged weeks, so those two steps are carrying full weight in that scenario — worth naming rather than leaving implicit.

**2. No step for "the plan didn't hold and there is a live overdose risk right now."**
The escalation sequence currently is: recognise the sign → freezer the phone → if still unsafe, call the crisis line. That's a reasonable ladder for de-escalating the urge, but it stops at the crisis line. There's no next step for the harder case: she's already taken medication, or is about to and can't wait for the line to answer, or the line doesn't get through. The plan needs an explicit final rung — emergency services (999/111/etc. as locally appropriate) or an equivalent "this is a medical emergency now" instruction — positioned as the step beyond the crisis line, not folded into it. Given prescribed medication is physically in the home during unflagged weeks, this isn't a hypothetical edge case.

### Feasibility check (per element, against the actual crisis state: intoxicated, late night, alone, post-breakup)

| Element | Feasible under actual crisis state? |
|---|---|
| Recognising "3+ drinks + texting ex" | Yes — behavioural, observable, matches established pattern |
| Crisis line as top contact | Yes — minimal cognitive/motor demand |
| Freezer phone method | Yes — friction-based, doesn't require willpower or memory of a plan |
| Sister holding medication | Yes, *but only for flagged weeks* — untested/unaddressed for unflagged/spontaneous risk |
| Calling crisis line over a friend | Yes — reasoning is sound and matches known availability gap with friends at 2am |
| What happens if none of this is enough | **Not addressed** — no named next step beyond the crisis line |

---

### Recommended additions (minimal, not a rewrite)

1. Add a same-day means-restriction option for unflagged weeks, or explicitly note that flagged-week removal is the only means-restriction layer and name what covers the gap.
2. Add an explicit final escalation line: if the crisis line doesn't resolve it, or danger becomes immediate (medication already taken or about to be), the next step is emergency services — named specifically, not implied.

### Collaboration status
Not established in the material provided — the plan reads as if self-authored/highly specific to the caller's own language and routines, which is a good sign of genuine engagement, but this should be confirmed rather than assumed.
