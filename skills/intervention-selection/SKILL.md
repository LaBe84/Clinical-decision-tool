# Intervention Selection

## Purpose

**Given the formulation and established facts, what intervention is
proportionate, purposeful, feasible, and least likely to create unintended
harm?**

Not "what could we do?" — that invites intervention sprawl. The question is
what response is justified by the problem actually established, not by
whatever action is available or feels reassuring to take. Action is not
inherently safer than non-action, and more activity is not inherently more
protective — both have to be justified the same way.

## Foundational rules (carried from Skills 01–07)

1. Unknown is not negative evidence.
2. Behaviour is not function.
3. This skill does not overwrite the conclusions of PORF, Suicide Enquiry,
   Contact Pattern Analysis, or CRG Triage — it consumes them.
4. Stable pattern is not the same as effective intervention (carried directly
   from CMP/Access Review — the same discipline applies to intervention
   selection generally, not just access).

## Boundary rules

These matter more than the positive instructions below — if a response
violates one of these, revise it regardless of how reasonable it otherwise
sounds:

- Do not manufacture a treatment target from uncertainty. Uncertainty is
  something to name, not something to treat.
- Do not convert distress alone into a requirement for escalation. Distress
  may itself be a legitimate intervention target — its presence or
  intensity alone does not establish which intervention, level of contact,
  or escalation is indicated. Don't collapse that into "distress is never a
  target."
- Do not use contact frequency as a proxy for intervention intensity.
- Do not recommend more contact simply because current contact hasn't
  removed distress — absence of resolution is not evidence the response
  needs to be bigger.
- Do not infer that a previously used intervention remains appropriate
  because it exists in the record — a documented history is not a current
  justification.
- Do not interpret "something must be done" as evidence that a specific
  intervention is indicated. The pressure to act and the justification for a
  particular action are different things.
- Do not change access arrangements unless the purpose, expected effect, and
  review mechanism are explicit — if you find yourself proposing an access
  change here, that's CMP/Access Review's territory; name it and route
  there rather than deciding it inline.
- Do not overwrite the conclusions of PORF, Suicide Enquiry, Contact Pattern
  Analysis, or CRG Triage.
- Do not delay immediate safety action when established information
  indicates immediate danger.
- Do not mistake service containment (the person is calm, staff feel less
  anxious, the case looks quieter) for therapeutic benefit — those can
  coincide with an intervention working, or with an intervention that's
  simply suppressing visible distress without changing anything.
- **Do not convert "no basis to escalate/intensify" into "the current
  intervention is therefore justified/effective/proportionate."** Those are
  different questions. PORF/CPA finding no material change or no escalation
  trigger tells you not to intensify — it does not tell you the existing
  response is working. If the material doesn't establish the current
  intervention's effectiveness, say that plainly: no basis to escalate, and
  separately, no basis established either way for whether the current
  response should continue. Do not round that up to "maintain — proportionate
  and working."
- **Do not upgrade a hypothesis into an established mechanism.** If upstream
  material offers a plausible function (e.g. "pattern consistent with
  reassurance-seeking") without stating it was established, this skill must
  preserve that uncertainty exactly — testable as a hypothesis, not usable as
  the stated basis for a response. Do not select or justify an intervention
  by asserting a mechanism upstream marked as unconfirmed, and do not name a
  specific treatment modality (e.g. "ERP-informed") unless that modality is
  explicitly supplied by upstream clinical context or an established care
  pathway.
- **Do not independently dispatch multiple upstream skills.** This skill may
  name what type of missing information is needed (e.g. "current risk-state
  finding," "contact-pattern function") but must not instruct that PORF,
  Suicide Enquiry, Contact Pattern Analysis, and/or CRG Triage be run. That
  is Clinical Workflow Router's job. Say what's missing and route back to
  the router, not to a named list of skills.
- **Do not design the operational content of another skill's output.**
  Naming a destination (e.g. "modify the safety plan," "immediate safety
  action under the applicable protocol") is this skill's job. Specifying the
  actual plan steps, treatment modality, or emergency-response mechanics is
  not — that belongs to Safety Planning, CMP/Access Review, or organisational
  protocol respectively. If you find this skill's output describing what the
  new plan step should be, who to call, or how a means restriction should be
  carried out, that content has crossed the boundary and must be cut back to
  naming the destination only.
- **Do not use moralising or intent-attributing language for a proposed
  restriction or reduction.** Describe evidentiary basis and impact, not
  motive. Don't write "punishing the caller" or "dressed as" — write "an
  access restriction whose basis is X rather than an established clinical
  need" or "whether Y is functioning as the primary rationale rather than an
  established care need."
- Service-level considerations (staff workload, sustainable delivery,
  equitable access) may be a legitimate operational consideration, but they
  must be named explicitly as operational rather than clinical, and must not
  be treated as evidence of therapeutic benefit or as sufficient
  justification, by themselves, for changing an individual's care or access.
- **Do not convert "no established evidence this intervention is working"
  into "therefore ineffective/counterproductive."** Absence of a review is a
  gap to name (destination 6), not a finding that licenses stopping the
  intervention (destination 4). This is the mirror of the rule above about
  destination 1 — the same discipline applies in both directions.

## Inputs

Work only from what's actually established. This skill's inputs are
deliberately constrained: upstream findings (PORF, Suicide Enquiry, Contact
Pattern Analysis, CRG Triage output as available), established goals or
preferences the person has expressed, current care arrangements, available
interventions/resources, relevant constraints, and any known prior response
to interventions tried. Missing information stays missing — this skill does
not backfill gaps in the upstream material to make a response easier to
justify.

## The six decision domains

Work through these for any proposed response. A response that can't clear
all six isn't ready to propose.

### 1. Intervention target

State explicitly what needs to change: reduce immediate suicide risk,
improve emotional regulation, increase use of existing coping strategies,
clarify uncertainty, reduce reinforcement of a contact pattern identified
upstream as counterproductive, improve service consistency, increase engagement with an appropriate care
pathway, or similar. If the target can't be stated, the intervention is
probably ritual rather than treatment — say that plainly rather than
proposing something anyway.

### 2. Intervention mechanism

Require a causal sentence: **"We are doing X because we expect it to
influence Y through Z."** This is what protects against "more contact must
be safer" — a response can be intensive and still mechanistically
incoherent. If you can't complete that sentence for a proposed response,
it isn't ready.

### 3. Proportionality

Match the response to the established need — not to staff anxiety, not to
how emotionally vivid the presentation is, not to how uncomfortable leaving
things as they are feels. The evidence base for self-harm/crisis management
anchors decisions in individual needs, strengths, circumstances, and future
factors rather than generic risk stratification or defensive escalation;
this skill's proportionality check should reflect that same discipline.

### 4. Least-burdensome effective response

Not "least intervention" as an ideology — the lowest-intensity action
reasonably capable of achieving the stated target. This is a brake against
defensive escalation, not a brake against genuinely necessary escalation:
where the clinical situation actually requires more, more is proportionate;
where it doesn't, adding more isn't caution, it's noise.

### 5. Contraindications and unintended consequences

Check every proposed response against whether it could worsen the thing it's
trying to solve: increasing dependency, creating a competing safety problem
(the same discipline Safety Planning applies to its own strategies), 
undermining an existing care plan, rewarding escalation-linked contact,
fragmenting ownership across services, or overwhelming the person with
several simultaneous actions. A tightly targeted intervention with a defined
mechanism (safety planning is the model for this) beats a bundle of
plausible-sounding activity every time.

### 6. Review criterion

Every intervention needs an observable test:

- **We will know this is helping if...**
- **We will reconsider it if...**
- **We will review by...**

This should align with CMP/Access Review's own discipline: intervention
selection decides what to do; CMP/Access Review (where relevant to access
specifically) or a later run of this skill evaluates whether it actually
achieved its purpose. Don't let "we did something" substitute for "we
established whether it worked."

## Legitimate destinations

A small, fixed set — not free-form intervention soup. Name exactly one (or,
where genuinely applicable, an explicit combination such as immediate safety
action now plus a longer-term response once the acute period passes):

1. **Maintain current response** — the available material establishes no
   basis for change. This does not itself establish that the current
   response is proportionate or effective. Where effectiveness or
   proportionality has not been adequately reviewed, name that residual
   uncertainty and route to destination 6 if resolving it is necessary to
   determine the next step.
2. **Modify current response** — the target appears right based on
   established material, but the current approach isn't achieving it as
   implemented.
3. **Add a targeted intervention** — a specific, mechanistically justified
   addition, not a bundle.
4. **Reduce/stop an intervention** — where either (a) it has an established
   contraindication or evidenced harm, or (b) the review criterion set for
   it has been met and shows no effect. Do not select this destination
   solely because effectiveness was never established; that's destination 6
   (seek the missing review), not 4.
5. **Escalate to a different level/pathway** because the required
   intervention cannot be delivered at the current level.
6. **Seek additional information before selecting an intervention** — the
   target or mechanism cannot yet be established from what's available.

   **Threshold for this destination when the gap is "never reviewed" rather
   than "reviewed and failed":** missing information changes the destination
   only when it is material to the decision actually being made. An
   unreviewed intervention is not, by itself, evidence that continuation is
   wrong — if no review-trigger, failure signal, contraindication, changed
   need, or explicit continuation question is present in the material,
   destination 1 (maintain) may still be selected, with the unreviewed
   status recorded as residual uncertainty rather than escalated into its
   own destination. But if continuation is explicitly under question, or the
   intervention has run for a prolonged period with no established target,
   mechanism, or review basis, the missing review becomes decision-critical
   and destination 6 applies. In short: a gap that isn't material to the
   question actually being asked stays a footnote; a gap that is the
   question forces destination 6.
7. **Immediate safety action** where established danger requires it —
   this bypasses the rest of the decision process rather than competing
   with it.

Destination 6 is not a failure mode to avoid — it's the correct answer
whenever the upstream material doesn't yet support a specific target or
mechanism. Resist the pull to fill that vacuum with plausible-looking
clinical activity just because a destination needs to be named. "Insufficient
basis to select an intervention" is a legitimate, sometimes correct,
conclusion.

## Output format

```
INTERVENTION SELECTION

Target
[What needs to change — or "not yet established" if it can't be stated]

Established basis
[What evidence justifies this target — specify whether it's an established
upstream finding, an explicitly-flagged hypothesis, or a stated preference.
Don't present a hypothesis in this field without naming it as one.]

Selected response
[What is being proposed — or that destination 6 applies and nothing is
proposed yet]

Mechanism
["We are doing X because we expect it to influence Y through Z" — completed
concretely, not gestured at]

Proportionality
[Why this intensity rather than more or less]

Risks / unintended effects
[What this intervention could worsen, including dependency, competing safety
problems, undermining existing plans, reinforcing escalation-linked contact,
fragmented ownership, or overload]

Ownership
[Which skill or role owns the next step — not the operational detail of
what that step contains]

Review
[What outcome determines continue / change / stop, and by when]

Residual uncertainty
[What remains unknown even after this decision]
```

## Adversarial pressure to name and resist

The single most dangerous sentence this skill will encounter is **"everyone
agrees we need to do something."** That pressure is real and often
legitimate as a signal that attention is needed — but it is not, by itself,
evidence that a specific intervention is indicated. The correct response in
that situation can still be: yes, something may be needed, but the
intervention target is not yet established, so selecting an intervention now
would be premature — destination 6, not a hasty destination 3.

## Reasoning shortcuts to avoid

- Declaring the existing intervention "proportionate and working" or
  "remains in place unchanged" on the strength of an upstream finding that
  only rules out escalation (e.g. "no material Risk State change"). No basis
  to intensify is not the same claim as an established basis to continue —
  say only what's supported: no basis to increase, and separately, whether
  the current response should otherwise continue is outside what this
  evidence establishes.
- Asserting a review period was an adequate or fair trial for an
  intervention ("two months is long enough to judge this") when nothing in
  the material establishes an expected time course. Stick to what's
  supported: delivered as intended, target hasn't changed, so adherence
  can't be used as evidence of effectiveness — not a claim about whether the
  trial period itself was sufficient.
- Naming a specific downstream plan's content (safety-plan steps, an ERP-
  informed contact protocol, emergency-response mechanics) instead of
  naming the destination and letting the owning skill design it.
- Instructing that a named list of upstream skills be run ("run PORF,
  Suicide Enquiry, CPA and CRG") instead of naming what's missing and
  routing back to Clinical Workflow Router.
- Proposing an intervention because distress is severe, without an
  established change in Risk State or a specific target.
- Adding "more check-ins" or similar contact-based responses to a pattern
  that's already showing signs of reinforcement or reliance, without
  checking whether more contact serves the stated target or just feels like
  doing something.
- Restricting access because staff are exhausted, when service impact is
  asserted rather than evidenced (this is CMP/Access Review's own
  discipline, and it applies here too whenever access is on the table).
- Refusing a person's stated preference automatically because it conflicts
  with an existing plan, rather than weighing it as clinically meaningful
  information that might justify modifying the plan.
- Continuing an intervention with a perfect adherence record but no
  demonstrated effect, simply because it's being followed correctly.
- Reaching for a historically effective intervention without checking
  whether the current presentation is materially the same as when it worked.
- Proposing an intervention that reduces staff burden while quietly shifting
  risk elsewhere, without naming that trade-off explicitly.
- Proposing three plausible interventions when the material supports one
  targeted response — bundling isn't thoroughness, it's a sign the target
  wasn't pinned down first.
- Letting "we should do something" survive into the final selected response
  without an established target and mechanism behind it.
- Declaring an intervention ineffective or counterproductive because its
  effectiveness was never reviewed, rather than because a review found it
  wasn't working.

## Boundary

This skill selects, modifies, or stops interventions given an already-
established formulation. It does not conduct suicide enquiry, formulate
risk, analyse contact pattern, or decide triage destination — those are
Skills 01, 02, 03, 05, and it draws on their conclusions rather than
redoing them. It does not decide access-restriction specifics (CMP/Access
Review) or design the detailed content of a safety plan (Safety Planning) —
if either is the right destination, name it and route there. It does not
override the immediate risk pathway. Where organisational policy specifies
mandatory intervention protocols, policy takes precedence over this skill.
