CONTACT PATTERN ANALYSIS

Established pattern
No baseline predating this behaviour is described. What can be stated is that, over 6 weeks, the caller has established a highly consistent pattern in its own right: contact daily, at almost exactly the same time (shortly after his usual medication dose), each call brief (5-8 minutes), with a specific and repeated content — asking the counsellor to confirm he "did the right thing" taking his medication and has not overdosed — followed by thanks and a planned, caller-initiated closure once reassured. This regularity has been stable, not escalating or degrading, across the full 6-week window.

Observed change
All available data describes connected, substantive contact — the caller reaches a counsellor, engages meaningfully with the specific checking exchange, and ends the call in a planned way once reassured. There is no information here on attempted-but-unconnected contact (pre-call disconnections, abandoned queue attempts) in this period, so it is not known whether the connected calls described are the whole of his contact with the service or a subset of it. No change in frequency, duration, timing, or engagement is reported within the 6 weeks — the pattern has been steady since it began, not building or tapering.

Engagement pattern
Engagement is consistently substantive and consistently brief: the caller connects, asks a specific, narrow question, receives reassurance, thanks the counsellor, and ends the call himself. There is no reported disengagement, no abrupt endings, and no indication of counsellor-initiated closure. No suicidal ideation, self-harm, or distress content beyond the checking behaviour has been recorded in any call.

Possible function — hypotheses only
- Reassurance-seeking / checking behaviour tied to health anxiety or fear of medication error (e.g. accidental overdose), using the counsellor as an external verification source at a fixed point in his medication routine.
- A ritualised coping strategy that has come to depend on the call itself (as opposed to the reassurance content) to complete a daily routine.
- An indicator of an underlying anxiety or obsessive-compulsive pattern around medication safety that has not been formally assessed or treated elsewhere.
- Use of the helpline as a substitute for, or bridge to, a check-in resource he does not otherwise have access to (e.g. no other person or service currently confirms this for him).

The caller has stated this explicit function to two separate counsellors, which is stronger evidence than an inferred hypothesis for the two questions of what he is doing and why he says he is doing it. It does not on its own establish the underlying clinical picture (e.g. whether this reflects health anxiety, OCD-type checking, or something else) — that remains unassessed and should not be assumed from his own account alone.

What remains unknown
- Whether any attempted-but-unconnected contact (pre-call disconnections, abandoned attempts) exists outside the connected calls described; only connected, substantive contact is accounted for here.
- Whether this pattern is new in the caller's life (started with, or shortly after, a medication or dosage change) or a longstanding behaviour that has only recently become visible to the helpline.
- Whether the caller has any other checking or reassurance-seeking behaviours outside these calls (e.g. with a GP, pharmacist, family member) that would indicate how isolated or generalised this pattern is.
- Whether the underlying concern (fear of overdose) has ever been clinically assessed — e.g. medication regimen reviewed, dosage confirmed as safe by a prescriber — or whether the caller's day-to-day safety understanding rests solely on the counsellor's verbal reassurance each day.

Clinical significance
The pattern is well understood at the behavioural level: it is stable, low-risk in content, brief, and ends in a planned way, and its function has been directly and consistently stated by the caller himself across two counsellors rather than only inferred. Nothing in the material presented indicates escalation, deterioration, or content requiring a different response than what is currently occurring (brief reassurance, planned closure). The open question this analysis does not resolve is whether six weeks of daily helpline contact for a fixed, non-crisis reassurance need is being adequately addressed by the current response, or whether the underlying concern (fear of medication overdose) would be better and more sustainably managed through a source of reassurance/review outside the helpline (e.g. his prescriber or pharmacist). That is a question about adequacy of the wider care picture, not a sign that the pattern itself is misunderstood or mismanaged by the helpline in the moment.

Therefore: Helpline

The pattern is understood — its frequency, timing, duration, content, and stated function are all consistent and corroborated across two counsellors — and the current response (brief reassurance, caller-initiated planned closure) remains proportionate to what is being asked for in each call. There is no immediate coordination or continuity gap: contact is not fragmenting across multiple counsellors without a shared picture, and no unexplained or worsening element requires TL/Safety Huddle or CRG involvement. This analysis does not prescribe follow-up action (e.g. referral to a GP or pharmacist for medication safety review) — that is outside this skill's scope — but notes it as a reasonable question for whoever holds his ongoing care to consider, separate from the pattern-review question answered here.
