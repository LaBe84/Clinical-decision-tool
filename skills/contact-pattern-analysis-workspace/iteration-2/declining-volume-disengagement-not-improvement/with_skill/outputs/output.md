CONTACT PATTERN ANALYSIS

Established pattern
For several months, this caller contacted Lifeline 3–4 times a week, consistently engaging well and using the support to manage chronic distress. This is a genuine, well-established baseline: consistent frequency, consistent engagement, and a known purpose (management of chronic distress).

Observed change
Over the last 3 weeks, contact frequency has dropped from 3–4 times a week to once every 10 days — a large and sustained reduction from baseline, not a single unusual night. The two most recent calls were shorter than the caller's usual pattern, and both ended the same way: the caller said "never mind, it doesn't matter" and hung up before the counsellor could explore further.

This description is limited to connected, substantive-attempt contact as reported. No information is available on whether there have been additional attempted-but-unconnected contacts (e.g. calls terminated during a pre-call message, abandoned in a queue) during this period. If that data exists, it should be checked before treating the picture above as complete — an unseen layer of attempted contact could change the read on what's happening.

Engagement pattern
Engagement has changed in kind, not just in volume. Previously the caller engaged well throughout contact. In the two most recent calls, engagement dropped away partway through, and the caller pre-empted exploration by minimising ("it doesn't matter") and disconnecting before the counsellor could ask anything further. This is a change in how contact is used, not simply a change in how often it happens.

Possible function — hypotheses only
Function is not established from the available information. Nothing in the material indicates what has changed for this person or why contact has become both less frequent and more abruptly terminated. Generating a list of plausible explanations (e.g. loss of trust in the service, increased risk, an unrelated life change, reduced distress) would create the appearance of formulation without adding anything the evidence actually supports, so none is offered here. The one thing the behaviour itself indicates, at the observational level, is that on the last two occasions the caller approached but did not complete a disclosure ("never mind, it doesn't matter") — this is an observed interaction pattern, not a stated reason, and should not be read as evidence of any specific underlying function.

What remains unknown
- Why frequency has dropped and stayed lower for three weeks.
- What "never mind, it doesn't matter" refers to on either occasion.
- Whether there have been any attempted-but-unconnected contacts in this window.
- Whether anything relevant has changed in this person's circumstances, supports, or risk status.
- Whether the shorter calls and early endings reflect the same underlying issue each time, or two separate, unrelated moments.

Clinical significance
Two separate questions apply here, and neither is answered yet.

Is the pattern explained by the existing formulation? No. The existing formulation understands this person's contact as a way of managing chronic distress through frequent, well-engaged contact. The current pattern — markedly reduced frequency, shorter calls, and disconnection before exploration on the last two occasions — is not accounted for by that formulation as it stands. This is a sustained change (three weeks, not an isolated night) and it is unexplained, which is the specific condition that warrants checking whether the formulation still holds.

Is the current response achieving its purpose? On the two most recent contacts, no — counsellors have twice attempted to explore what's changed and been unable to, because the caller ended the call first. That is not evidence the supportive-contact approach is failing overall, but it is evidence that, on the two most recent occasions, the intervention did not reach the point of finding out what it needed to find out. A drop in volume paired with early, avoidant disconnection is not evidence of resolution — declining contact is not, by itself, evidence of improvement, and here it is compounded by a change in how the caller is disengaging mid-conversation.

Therefore
CRG.

This is not a case of persistence alone, and not a visibility/coordination problem — the pattern is not split across multiple counsellors or channels in a way that's obscuring the picture; it has already been observed clearly and consistently by whoever took the last two calls. The trigger here is that the change is sustained (three weeks), the pattern and its function remain genuinely unexplained after the reasonable review the record allows, and the existing formulation's core assumption — that frequent contact is doing the work of managing this person's chronic distress — no longer clearly matches what's being observed, given that contact has dropped and the caller is now disengaging before the counsellor can explore what's happening. Twice being unable to complete exploration is itself relevant: it means the gap in understanding hasn't been closable through the ordinary course of the calls themselves.

Directly on the manager's question: no, the drop in volume should not be read as improvement. Reduced contact is not evidence of improvement on its own, and here it is accompanied by shorter calls and a new pattern of pre-emptive disconnection before disclosure — a change in the quality of engagement, not just its quantity. That combination is more consistent with disengagement than with resolution, but this analysis does not establish which it is; that determination sits with CRG, informed by whatever additional history and, where appropriate, formulation review follows from there.
