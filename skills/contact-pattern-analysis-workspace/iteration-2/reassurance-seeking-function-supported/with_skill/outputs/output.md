CONTACT PATTERN ANALYSIS

Established pattern
No baseline exists from before this behaviour started. What is available describes only the six weeks now on record, and within that window the pattern has itself been stable and consistent: daily contact, occurring at almost exactly the same time each day (shortly after his usual medication dose), each call brief (5–8 minutes), following the same shape — he asks the counsellor to confirm he "did the right thing" taking his medication and that he hasn't overdosed, receives reassurance, thanks the counsellor, and ends the call. There is no record of what contact (if any) looked like before this six-week pattern began, so it cannot be said whether this represents a change from an earlier pattern or is the only pattern on record for this person.

Observed change
Within the six-week window there is no material change to characterise — frequency, timing, duration, and call content have remained consistent throughout. The data available is connected, substantive contact (calls that reached a counsellor and were engaged with, briefly). There is no information here on attempted-but-unconnected contact (calls terminated before reaching a counsellor, abandoned in queue, or otherwise not captured) — this is a limitation on the picture, not evidence that no such attempts exist. Any conclusion below is based on the connected-contact subset only.

Engagement pattern
Engagement is brief and tightly scripted rather than open-ended: he raises a specific, consistent question (confirmation that he took his medication correctly and has not overdosed), the counsellor responds, he thanks them, and the call ends — a pattern that has repeated in essentially the same form daily for six weeks. No suicidal ideation, self-harm, or distress content beyond this specific checking behaviour has been recorded in any call.

Possible function — hypotheses only
This is not a hypothesis-list case. The function is directly evidenced: he has told two separate counsellors, in consistent terms, exactly what he is calling for and why — post-dose reassurance-seeking, checking that he has taken his medication safely and not overdosed. Per the skill's distinction, this is described here as the observed function at the behavioural level, not as one hypothesis among several. This does not, however, amount to a full clinical explanation of the underlying need driving the checking behaviour (e.g. why medication-taking specifically has become a focus of this level of doubt) — that remains outside what contact-pattern analysis alone can establish.

What remains unknown
- Whether any pattern existed before this six-week window, and if so what it looked like.
- Whether there have been unconnected or pre-call-disconnected attempts alongside the six weeks of connected contact recorded here.
- Whether the underlying medication-safety doubt/checking behaviour has a formulation elsewhere (e.g. GP, mental health team, existing safety or care plan) that this contact pattern should be read against.
- Whether frequency, duration, or content has shifted at all over the six weeks (the material as given describes a static pattern, but this should be confirmed against the full log rather than assumed).

Clinical significance
Two separate questions apply here, and they don't have the same answer.
Is the pattern explained? Yes — better explained than most cases this skill sees. The function is directly and consistently evidenced by his own account to two counsellors, not inferred.
Is the existing response achieving its purpose, or maintaining the cycle it responds to? This is the open question. Six weeks of daily, same-time, same-script reassurance that resolves cleanly every time is a stable pattern, not, by itself, evidence that reassurance is resolving anything. A post-dose reassurance-seeking pattern that has neither reduced in frequency nor changed in form after six weeks of the same response being given daily is consistent with the reassurance itself maintaining the checking behaviour rather than addressing it — the daily call may be functioning as the mechanism that lets the checking continue unchanged, rather than as an intervention resolving it. That reading is not established as fact from contact pattern alone, but the persistence of an identical cycle for six weeks straight is enough to make it a live question rather than a reassuring sign.

Therefore
CRG.

The pattern is well explained and there is no immediate coordination/visibility problem within the six-week window itself (the same behaviour has been consistently reported, not scattered incoherently across disconnected fragments). What is missing is evidence that the current response — daily reassurance — is achieving anything beyond running alongside the checking behaviour it responds to. With function this clearly evidenced and the pattern unchanged in frequency or form after six weeks of an identical response, the question of whether the intervention needs to change (for example, whether the underlying medication-safety anxiety needs addressing directly rather than met with a fresh reassurance cycle each day) should go to CRG. This is not triggered by frequency or persistence alone — it follows specifically because the intervention does not appear to be moving the underlying need, only meeting it daily in the same way. Separately, since this has now surfaced independently to two different counsellors, it is also worth confirming that the existing formulation or care record for this caller has actually consolidated this information rather than relying on it being noticed again by chance.
