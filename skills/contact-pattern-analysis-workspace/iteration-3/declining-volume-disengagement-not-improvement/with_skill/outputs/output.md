CONTACT PATTERN ANALYSIS

Established pattern
This caller has an established baseline of 3-4 contacts per week, sustained
over several months. Within that baseline, contact was consistently
connected and substantively engaged — the material describes the caller as
using support to manage chronic distress in a way that reads as effective
engagement with the service, not just high volume. No information is given
about typical call duration, time of day, whether the caller used
planned/scheduled contact, whether they stayed with one counsellor or
switched, or whether emergency escalation had occurred previously — these
are gaps in the baseline description, not findings.

Observed change
Over the last 3 weeks, connected contact frequency has dropped from
3-4 times a week to once every 10 days. This is a substantial reduction at
the connected-contact level. Within that reduced frequency, the last two
calls were shorter than the caller's usual pattern and ended with the
caller saying "never mind, it doesn't matter" before hanging up, before the
counsellor could explore further — i.e., counsellor-interrupted or
caller-initiated abrupt endings rather than planned closures.

No data is available on attempted contact (calls that did not connect,
pre-call disconnections, abandoned queue attempts) during this three-week
period. The pattern described here is therefore a pattern in connected
contact only. If attempted-but-unconnected contact exists and hasn't been
reviewed, the true picture of this caller's help-seeking behaviour over
the last three weeks may look different from what connected-call data
alone shows — this is stated as a limitation, not resolved one way or the
other.

Engagement pattern
Where contact has occurred in the last three weeks, engagement appears to
have declined within the call itself: shorter calls, and a repeated
abrupt-ending behaviour ("never mind, it doesn't matter," then hanging up)
before the counsellor could explore what prompted the call or what "it"
refers to. This has now occurred on the last two contacts, which is enough
to describe as a repeating within-call pattern rather than a single
isolated instance — but it is still only two data points, and there is no
information on what preceded the calls, what "it" refers to, or how the
counsellor responded in the moments before disconnection.

Possible function — hypotheses only
Function is not established from available information. The caller has
not stated, directly or consistently, what the reduced contact or the
abrupt endings are for. The phrase "never mind, it doesn't matter" is
compatible with several different readings — including withdrawal,
diminished belief that the call will help, difficulty articulating
something, or something unrelated to the caller's underlying distress —
and nothing in the material points toward any one of these over the
others. No hypothesis is offered here beyond noting that multiple
directions remain equally plausible on the information available.

What remains unknown
- Attempted-contact data for the last three weeks (pre-call
  disconnections, abandoned attempts) — not provided, so the true change
  in this caller's total help-seeking behaviour cannot be assessed from
  connected-call data alone.
- What "it" refers to in "never mind, it doesn't matter," and what
  preceded that statement on each of the last two calls.
- Any change in the caller's external circumstances, mental state, or
  support network over the three-week window — none is described.
- Whether the reduced frequency reflects reduced need, reduced capacity or
  willingness to reach out, reduced trust in the service, or something
  else entirely.
- Whether this is the start of a sustained trajectory or could still
  revert — three weeks of change following several months of a stable
  baseline is a meaningful duration, but there is no data point beyond the
  most recent call to show whether the trend is continuing, stabilising,
  or reversing.

Clinical significance
The pattern is not adequately explained by the existing formulation: there
is no established account, from this caller or from clinical review, of
why contact has dropped or why the last two calls have ended abruptly. The
change is both a reduction in frequency and a change in within-call
engagement, sustained across the last three weeks rather than a single
occasion, which is enough to treat as more than an isolated variation —
but "sustained and unexplained" is not by itself a finding of
deterioration or improvement. It only establishes that the current
understanding of this caller's contact pattern no longer matches what is
happening, and that the two most recent contacts ended before the
counsellor could establish what changed.

The reduced-frequency change and the abrupt-ending change may be related
or may reflect separate things happening for this caller; the material
does not allow them to be treated as one confirmed presentation, only as
two changes that should be reviewed together for continuity.

Therefore
The pattern and its function are genuinely unexplained after the
information available, and the two most recent contacts have each ended
before that gap could be closed by the counsellor — this is not a
visibility/coordination problem (the picture is not split across
disconnected records; it is a single, coherent, but unexplained change
in one caller's contact pattern), and it does not meet the threshold of
"adequately explained, with the response demonstrably working" needed to
conclude Helpline is sufficient. → CRG.

This reflects an oversight-escalation need — a persisting, unexplained
change in an established pattern — not a determination that the caller's
risk has increased or that anything has improved. What the reduction in
contact frequency means for this individual is not established by this
analysis and should not be characterised as either a positive or a
concerning sign on the basis of volume alone; the material leaves that
question open, and it should be formulated, not assumed, in the next
stage of review.
