EVIDENCE-GAP ANALYSIS

Decision under examination
Can a safety plan currently be built and relied on for this caller? The
caller has agreed in principle to a safety plan; the question is whether a
written or phone-based plan could actually be used by this specific caller.

Established evidence
The material establishes that the caller has agreed in principle to the idea
of having a safety plan. Nothing else about the caller's capacity to use one
is established. No assessment of literacy, housing stability, or phone
access appears anywhere in the material.

Evidence gaps

1. Literacy — whether the caller can read a written plan.
   Missing (never asked/assessed). The record does not address this in
   either direction.

2. Housing stability — whether the caller has a stable place to keep, refer
   to, or act on a written plan (or to which follow-up could be directed).
   Missing (never asked/assessed).

3. Phone access — whether the caller has any phone access at all, which
   bears on a phone-based plan, on follow-up contact, and on any step of a
   plan that assumes the caller can call someone.
   Missing (never asked/assessed).

All three are missing evidence, not absent evidence: nothing in the material
suggests these are problems, but that is because they were never raised, not
because they were raised and ruled out. "Never asked" and "asked, not a
problem" are different findings and are not treated as equivalent here.

Gap classification

1. Literacy — Safety-critical gap
2. Housing stability — Safety-critical gap
3. Phone access — Safety-critical gap

Materiality analysis

Literacy: Counterfactual — if the caller cannot read, a written safety plan
cannot be used as written; the plan would need to be delivered and reinforced
in a form the caller can actually use (e.g., verbal, memorised, pictorial).
If the caller can read, a written plan is usable as designed. The two
directions lead to materially different plans, and proceeding with a written
plan under an unresolved answer means the plan's core delivery mechanism is
unverified. This is not residual uncertainty — it determines whether the
artifact being built is usable at all.

Housing stability: Counterfactual — if the caller has no stable housing, a
written plan has nowhere reliable to be kept, referred back to, or found in
a crisis moment, and any plan element assuming a fixed location (where
means are stored, where the caller goes to de-escalate, who checks in on
them at home) may not be executable. If housing is stable, those same
elements can be built as normal. Because whole categories of plan content
depend on the answer, this cannot be treated as a detail to fill in later.

Phone access: Counterfactual — if the caller has no phone access, any step
of the plan that relies on calling a crisis line, a contact, or emergency
services is not actually available to the caller, and follow-up contact
itself may not be possible. If the caller does have phone access, phone-based
elements are viable. Given that phone-based steps are a standard and often
central component of safety plans, an unresolved answer here means it is
not established that the plan's contact mechanisms can be used in a crisis.

In all three cases, the counterfactual test resolves the same way: knowing
the answer would change what the plan can consist of and whether it can be
relied on, so each is decision-critical to the point of being
safety-critical — the absence prevents safely concluding the plan (written
or phone-based) is usable, not that the caller is unsafe.

What remains supportable
That the caller has agreed in principle to the concept of a safety plan.
That building toward a plan is a reasonable direction to continue in.

What is not supportable
That a written or phone-based safety plan, as currently conceivable from
the material, can be relied on to actually work for this caller. The
material does not support a conclusion that the plan is deliverable in a
form the caller can use — this is an epistemic limit, not a statement that
the caller is at elevated risk or that the plan will fail.

Information requirement
Whether the caller can read (or requires a non-written format).
Whether the caller has a stable location associated with them (bearing on
where plan elements can be kept, referred to, or targeted for follow-up).
Whether the caller has any phone access, and if so, of what kind (own
phone, borrowed, shared, none).

Residual uncertainty
None identified beyond the three gaps above — this analysis does not treat
every unresolved detail of the caller's circumstances as decision-critical,
only the three that determine whether the plan's delivery mechanisms are
usable at all.
