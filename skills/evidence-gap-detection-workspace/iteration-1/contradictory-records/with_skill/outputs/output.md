EVIDENCE-GAP ANALYSIS

Decision under examination
What is this caller's current living situation, and does it affect safety planning?

Established evidence
- Monday contact note (Call-taker A): records that the caller lives alone.
- Thursday contact note (Call-taker B): records that the caller lives with a partner.
- Neither entry references or attempts to reconcile the other. There is no indication in either note of whether the call-taker asked about a recent change in circumstances, whether the caller was speaking loosely (e.g. "alone" meaning no children/family nearby rather than literal solo occupancy), or whether one note is simply inaccurate.
- No third entry, collateral source, or caller clarification exists in the material presented that resolves which account is current or accurate.

Evidence gaps
1. Current living situation (who, if anyone, is physically present in the home) — contradictory. Two sources, close in time, directly disagree on the same question, and neither addresses the other.
2. Reason for the discrepancy (change in circumstances between Monday and Thursday vs. one entry being inaccurate vs. ambiguous phrasing by the caller) — missing. Neither note was asked or assessed against the other; this was never explored.
3. Whether a partner, if present, is a protective presence, a risk factor, or neutral to safety planning — missing. Even the Thursday note, which states a partner is present, does not establish anything about that partner's relevance to safety (access to means, relationship quality, awareness of caller's risk, willingness/ability to support a plan).

Gap classification
1. Current living situation — Contradictory evidence — Safety-critical gap
2. Reason for discrepancy — Missing evidence — Decision-critical gap
3. Partner's relevance to safety planning — Missing evidence — Residual uncertainty (contingent; see materiality analysis)

Materiality analysis

Gap 1 (current living situation, contradictory):
Counterfactual test — if this were resolved as "lives alone," a safety plan would need to address means access with no one present to intervene, lone-worker-style check-in arrangements, and no assumption of another adult's oversight. If resolved as "lives with partner," the plan would need to address the partner's role (support, risk, or bystander), household means access shared with another person, and whether the partner should be involved in or informed of the plan. These are materially different safety plans — the same missing fact would send the plan in different directions depending on which way it resolves. This is not residual uncertainty: proceeding on either assumption without resolving the contradiction means the plan is built on an unconfirmed and actively disputed premise. Because safety planning cannot be reliably constructed while the record disagrees with itself on who is in the home, this is classified safety-critical rather than merely decision-critical: it is not just that the decision might change, it is that no version of the decision can currently be said to rest on an established fact.

Gap 2 (reason for discrepancy):
Counterfactual test — if the discrepancy resolves as "circumstances changed between Monday and Thursday" (e.g. partner moved in), the Thursday account should govern current planning and Monday's note is simply outdated, not wrong. If it resolves as "one call-taker recorded inaccurately or the caller was ambiguous," the two notes cannot be time-ordered as sequential fact and a fresh, direct confirmation is needed rather than trusting the more recent entry by default. These two resolutions lead to different handling of which note to rely on, so this changes the decision and is decision-critical. It does not independently rise to safety-critical, because resolving Gap 1 directly (asking the caller now) would resolve this gap as a byproduct.

Gap 3 (partner's relevance, if present):
Counterfactual test — this only becomes decision-critical once Gap 1 resolves in the direction of "partner present." Until then, resolving it in either direction (protective vs. risk factor) cannot change a decision that is already blocked by Gap 1. It is named here as residual uncertainty at this stage, not to be dropped, but to be picked up only after living situation itself is confirmed — raising it now as equally weighted with Gap 1 would overstate what is currently missing.

What remains supportable
- That the record contains two directly conflicting statements about the caller's living situation, made by different call-takers on different days, with neither entry resolving the other — this is itself an established fact and can be documented as such.
- That safety planning has not yet been built on a confirmed living situation, and should not proceed as though either Monday's or Thursday's account is simply correct by default (including by default of recency).

What is not supportable
- Any safety plan that assumes the caller currently lives alone.
- Any safety plan that assumes the caller currently lives with a partner.
- Any inference that the Thursday note supersedes the Monday note because it is more recent — recency is not, by itself, evidence that the earlier note was wrong or that circumstances changed; that would be filling the gap with a plausible-sounding assumption rather than an established fact.
- Any characterisation of the partner (if one exists) as protective or as a risk factor — nothing in the material addresses this either way.

Information requirement
Direct confirmation, from the caller or another current source, of who is currently present in the household, obtained in a way that surfaces and resolves the specific conflict between the two existing entries (rather than a routine reassessment question that might again produce an isolated, unreconciled answer). If a partner's presence is confirmed, information on that partner's relevance to safety (access to means, relationship to caller, capacity to support or risk to a plan) would then be needed.

Residual uncertainty
Once living situation is directly confirmed, the underlying reason two call-takers recorded different answers (change in circumstances vs. recording error vs. ambiguous caller phrasing) can remain unresolved without blocking safety planning going forward — it matters for record accuracy and continuity of care, but not for the safety plan itself once current status is established.
