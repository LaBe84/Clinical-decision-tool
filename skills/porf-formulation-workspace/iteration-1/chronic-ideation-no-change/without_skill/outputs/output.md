# Formulation and Response: Weekly Caller, Chronic Suicidal Ideation, No Change Tonight

## Case Summary
34-year-old woman, known to the service for 3 years, contacts approximately weekly with chronic suicidal ideation she characterises as a stable background phenomenon. Tonight: ideation present but unchanged in intensity, no plan, no intent, no acute stressor, describes a "decent day," is calling to talk it through — consistent with her established, well-documented pattern. No prior acts of self-harm or suicide attempts in the 3-year contact history.

## Formulation

**Risk level: baseline/chronic, not acute.** The core clinical task here is differentiating chronic suicidal ideation (a longstanding trait-like feature of her presentation, often seen in complex trauma, personality-level difficulties, or chronic depression) from an acute suicidal crisis. Tonight's call has none of the markers that typically signal escalation:

- No change in ideation intensity, frequency, or quality
- No emergence of plan, means, or intent
- No new stressor, loss, or precipitant
- No change in protective factors
- Mood if anything is slightly better ("decent day")
- Help-seeking behaviour is itself protective and consistent with her adaptive coping style — she is using the service exactly as it is meant to be used

Three years of weekly contact without any act is a substantial protective data point. It suggests the calls function as an effective coping/regulation strategy for her, not as a crisis behaviour or a precursor to escalation. Treating a stable, chronic presentation as if it were a new acute risk episode every time would be both clinically inaccurate and counter-therapeutic — it risks teaching her that calm, honest disclosure of background ideation triggers heavy-handed intervention, which could paradoxically suppress future help-seeking or push her toward minimising symptoms to avoid escalation responses.

That said, chronic suicidal ideation is not zero risk, and "known to the service" does not mean "assume nothing has changed" — the formulation still requires an active check, not a passive assumption based on pattern-matching to prior calls.

## Proportionate Response

1. **Standard risk check-in, not a full crisis assessment.** Briefly and collaboratively confirm the absence of change: ask directly about plan, intent, means, and any new stressors, and listen for anything that contradicts her own framing. This should feel like a normal part of the supportive conversation, not an interrogation — but it must still happen explicitly rather than being skipped because "she said nothing's changed."

2. **Provide the supportive listening contact she is asking for.** This is the primary intervention tonight. Let her talk it through, validate the exhausting, chronic nature of living with background ideation, and reinforce that reaching out is the right and effective thing she is already doing.

3. **No escalation to emergency services, no imposed safety planning overhaul, no referral to crisis/acute pathways.** These would be disproportionate to presented risk and could damage the therapeutic relationship and her willingness to keep calling.

4. **Light-touch review of her existing safety plan**, if one exists and it fits naturally into the conversation — not because risk indicates it's needed tonight, but as routine maintenance, e.g., "does everything on your plan still feel right, or has anything changed?" Keep this brief.

5. **Document clearly and specifically**, not just "no change." Record: ideation confirmed present, no plan/intent/means, no new stressors, mood stable-to-good, consistent with established baseline pattern, supportive call provided, no escalation indicated. This protects both the caller's continuity of care and the record, and gives the next call-taker an accurate comparator.

6. **Close normally**, reinforcing the open door for future contact, rather than adding unusual instructions or follow-up that would signal (inaccurately) that tonight's call was treated as higher risk than it was.

## Key Clinical Principle
Chronic suicidal ideation requires ongoing monitoring, not repeated crisis response. The proportionate action is a genuine but brief risk check confirming stability, followed by the supportive contact the caller is asking for. Escalating a stable, well-known pattern to crisis-level intervention is itself a risk management error — it can erode trust, discourage future disclosure, and misallocate crisis resources away from callers in acute distress. The response should match the actual clinical picture presented tonight, informed but not overridden by the 3-year history.
