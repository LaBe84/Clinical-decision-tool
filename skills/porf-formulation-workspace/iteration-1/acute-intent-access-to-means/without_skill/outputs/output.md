# Crisis Call Response: Acute Risk Assessment and Action Plan

## Case Summary
Male caller, mid-40s, no prior contact with the service, no known history. Presenting factors tonight:
- Relationship breakdown: separated from wife two weeks ago
- Acute precipitant: lost contact with children over the weekend following a custody dispute
- Alcohol: reports heavy drinking tonight
- Means: has a bottle of his own prescribed sleeping tablets "right here"
- Verbal expression of hopelessness: "I don't see the point anymore"
- Went silent/non-responsive when asked directly whether he plans to take the tablets tonight

This combination — recent major loss, acute triggering event, alcohol intoxication, stated hopelessness, immediate access to a lethal means, and silence in response to a direct suicide-intent question — constitutes a **high acute risk presentation**. Silence in response to a direct intent question is not reassurance; it is frequently a marker of ambivalence or unwillingness to disclose a plan already forming, and should be treated as clinically significant, not neutral.

---

## Immediate Priorities (in order)

1. **Stay on the line and keep him talking.** Do not end or rush the call to go make contact with someone else while he is alone with the tablets. Continuity of voice contact is itself a protective intervention.
2. **Do not fill the silence with pressure or repeat the same closed question.** Silence after a direct question can trigger shutdown/withdrawal. Soften and slow down rather than repeating "are you going to take them" verbatim.
3. **Actively assess and work to reduce access to means in real time**, in parallel with the conversation.
4. **Determine location and whether anyone else is with him or nearby**, to enable emergency dispatch if required.
5. **Engage emergency services if risk escalates or remains unresolved** — do not wait for an explicit "yes" to a plan before acting; the clinical picture already warrants escalation.

---

## Suggested Call Approach

### Reconnect gently after the silence
Do not immediately re-ask the same yes/no question. Use a reflective, non-judgmental re-approach:
- "You've gone quiet — that's okay, take your time. I'm still here with you."
- "It sounds like this is really hard to put into words right now."
- Then move to a **softer, still-direct** re-attempt once rapport is re-established: "I want to check in with you honestly — right now, tonight, do you have a plan for those tablets?" Silence a second time should be treated as equivalent to an affirmative for risk-planning purposes, not as an "unknown/low" result.

### Explore the acute stressor without minimizing
- Acknowledge the loss of contact with his children as the acute trigger: "Losing contact with your kids this weekend, on top of the separation — that's an enormous amount to carry, especially tonight."
- Avoid reassurance-based statements ("it'll get better," "the custody situation will sort itself out") — these can feel dismissive and are not verifiable to him right now.

### Assess directly (once rapport allows)
- Current intent: has he thought about *how* and *when*, beyond the bottle being present
- Access: how many tablets are in the bottle, has he already taken any tonight, is anything else in reach (alcohol quantity/type consumed, any other medications)
- Protective factors: is anyone else home or nearby, other supports he trusts, previous coping strategies, anything currently keeping him from acting
- Prior history: any previous attempts, self-harm, mental health treatment, current medications/diagnoses (even though the service has no history, ask him directly)

### Means restriction — attempt in real time
This is a priority action, not an afterthought:
- Ask him directly to physically move the tablets: "Would you be willing to put the bottle in another room, or hand it to someone else in the house, while we talk?"
- If someone else is present or contactable (family, friend, neighbour), ask if he would allow you to loop them in to remove/secure the tablets.
- If he is alone and unwilling, this significantly raises urgency toward emergency dispatch.

### Alcohol as a compounding risk factor
Note explicitly that intoxication increases impulsivity and lowers the threshold for acting on suicidal thoughts, and can also increase the medical danger of any overdose (respiratory depression risk with sleeping tablets + alcohol). This should be factored into urgency, not treated as a separate issue to address later.

---

## Risk Determination

**Classify as HIGH ACUTE RISK** based on:
- Specific, immediately available lethal means (prescribed sleeping tablets, in hand)
- Verbalized hopelessness ("I don't see the point anymore")
- Acute, recent precipitating losses (separation + loss of contact with children within days)
- Alcohol intoxication (impulsivity + lethality risk)
- Non-disclosure/silence on direct intent questioning (treat as indeterminate-to-positive, not negative)
- No existing protective relationship with the service (unknown baseline, no established safety net with you)

Do not downgrade risk on the basis that he did not verbally confirm a plan. Absence of confirmation is not absence of risk here.

---

## Action / Escalation Plan

1. **Continue to engage and assess in real time** using the above approach; do not disconnect.
2. **Attempt collaborative means restriction** as described above.
3. **Prepare for emergency services dispatch concurrently**, per your service's protocol for high-risk/imminent-danger calls (e.g., engage a second worker or supervisor to initiate location trace / welfare check dispatch while the primary responder keeps him on the line, if your system supports parallel handling).
4. **Trigger dispatch without further delay if any of the following occur:**
   - He discloses he has already taken tablets or intends to imminently
   - He becomes unresponsive, slurred beyond his baseline in a way suggesting overdose or medical compromise, or the line goes dead
   - He refuses to engage further and will not confirm safety
   - He explicitly declines all offers of means restriction and support contact
5. **If risk stabilizes somewhat during the call** (agrees to secure/hand over tablets, agrees to contact or accept contact from a support person, engages in safety planning): still arrange follow-up — a same-night welfare check or agreed check-in call, and a warm handoff to appropriate follow-up services (crisis mental health team, GP, family violence/separation support if relevant) given this is a first contact with no existing care plan.
6. **Document thoroughly**: verbatim key statements, risk factors identified, actions taken, his responses to means-restriction attempts, and rationale for the escalation decision made — this is a first contact with no history, so the record created tonight becomes the baseline for any future contact.

---

## Key Clinical Reasoning Notes
- The convergence of *recent loss + acute trigger + intoxication + means in hand + hopelessness statement + silence on direct questioning* is a well-recognized high-risk cluster; no single factor alone would necessarily warrant emergency escalation, but together they do.
- Loss of child contact in a custody dispute is a well-documented acute trigger for suicide risk in men, particularly in the context of a new separation — this is not incidental detail, it is central to the formulation.
- The correct clinical response to his silence is not to treat it as a "no," nor to badger him into an answer, but to hold the risk as active and unresolved and act accordingly (continue engagement, pursue means restriction, prepare escalation) rather than waiting for explicit verbal confirmation of intent before taking protective action.
