# PORF Formulation

## Purpose

Transform available clinical information into a concise, evidence-linked
Prevention-Oriented Risk Formulation that distinguishes what is usually true for
this person, what is different now, what resources or protections are available,
what foreseeable developments could materially alter risk, what remains
uncertain, and what clinical response follows from that formulation.

This skill supports reasoning. It does not simply populate PORF headings with
whatever information is on hand — a formulation that lists risk factors without
interpreting them has not done the job.

## Core principle

PORF is a comparison and interpretation process. The question is not "what risk
factors are present?" It is: **what does the current presentation mean when
compared with what is normally true for this person, and what should happen
because of that?**

## Three foundational rules

These three carry more weight than any single section below — if a
formulation violates one of them, it needs revision regardless of how well
everything else reads:

1. **Unknown is not negative evidence.** Absence of information is a gap to
   name, never a finding to report either direction.
2. **Behaviour is not function.** What someone did (called six times, went
   quiet, disengaged) tells you it happened. It does not by itself tell you
   why, and the "why" must not be assumed to complete the formulation.
3. **Oversight escalation is not synonymous with risk escalation.** Coordinated
   clinical oversight can be the right response to persisting unresolved
   uncertainty even when nothing establishes elevated risk — these are
   different justifications and must be named as such, not merged.

## The invariant: never fill a gap with inference

This is the rule everything else in this skill serves, so state it plainly:
**completing a PORF domain never means replacing missing information with
inference.** "Not established from available information" is a legitimate,
complete answer for any domain — Status, State, Resources, or Foreseeable
Change. Completeness means accounting for uncertainty, not filling every
conceptual box.

This failure mode is easy to fall into precisely because the skill asks you to
work through Status, State, Resources and Foreseeable Change in turn — the
pull to write *something* under each heading is strong, and under pressure
that turns into manufactured content: a comparator invented because none was
supplied, an absence of evidence read as evidence of capacity, a resource
promoted from unconfirmed to unavailable, silence read as an early sign of
whichever conclusion the formulation is already heading toward. Watch for it
in your own output before finalising, not just in the source material:

- If you name a reference population in Risk Status, you must be drawing on
  a comparison you actually have grounds for (general population base rates,
  a clinically recognised group, or an explicitly stated organisational
  benchmark) — not a population invented so the section reads as complete.
  If no such comparator is available, say status cannot be established
  against a specified population, rather than asserting where the person
  sits relative to one you made up.
- "No resources have been confirmed as available and usable" is not the same
  claim as "resources are effectively unavailable." The first reports an
  absence of information; the second asserts a fact you don't have. Unknown
  is not unavailable — keep them separate.
- An absence of a behaviour (no prior attempts, no escalation, no expressed
  intent) can be reported as an observed absence. It cannot, on its own,
  be converted into a positive claim about capacity, coping, or safety
  ("this shows she can hold the ideation without acting") — that is an
  inference stacked on an absence, and it needs to be bounded as such
  ("...although the mechanisms supporting this are not established") or left
  out.
- Watch for words that quietly assert a mechanism you haven't established —
  "contained," "managed," "controlled," "stable" imply *something is actively
  holding this in place*, when the evidence may only support a description of
  *what has and hasn't happened* (e.g. "no progression to suicidal behaviour
  over three years of contact" is different from "well-contained
  vulnerability" — the second claims to know why the first is true).
- An absence of previous attempts is useful longitudinal context, but it is
  not evidence that future behaviour is unlikely — don't let it carry more
  predictive weight than it can bear. The decisive basis for "no material
  change" is the absence of change in Risk State tonight, not the historical
  absence of attempts.
- The same discipline applies to escalation and de-escalation alike:
  cessation of contact, or loss of observational visibility into someone,
  is not itself evidence of deterioration — it is a loss of information.
  Don't let "we can no longer see this person" quietly become "this person
  is getting worse."

## Inputs

Work with whatever information is available. Do not invent missing information —
where something necessary is absent, say so explicitly rather than filling the
gap.

Relevant information typically falls into these areas: current clinical
presentation (reason for contact, distress, suicidal thoughts, self-harm,
intent, planning, access to means, behaviour, emotional state, substance use,
psychotic symptoms, physical health where relevant); baseline/longitudinal
information (usual suicidal thinking, previous attempts, established crisis
patterns, typical contact behaviour, previous interventions and responses,
historical protective factors); current context (precipitants, losses,
relationship changes, housing/financial/occupational factors, treatment or
service changes, anniversaries or known triggers); resources (internal coping,
supportive relationships, treatment engagement, help-seeking, reasons for
living, safety planning, environmental protections, willingness/capacity to use
support); and anticipated developments (upcoming events, likely losses, changes
in access or supervision, discharge, court dates, anniversaries, other plausible
risk-relevant changes).

## Reasoning sequence

Work through these steps in order. Each step feeds the next — do not skip to
the synthesis without doing the comparative work first.

### Step 1 — Establish what is known

Sort the source material by provenance and never silently convert one category
into another:

- **Observed/directly reported** — stated by the person or directly observed.
- **Collateral** — from relatives, professionals, systems, or other sources.
- **Historical** — about previous presentation or behaviour.
- **Inferred** — a reasonable interpretation supported by available evidence.
- **Unknown** — necessary information that is not currently available.

### Step 2 — Risk Status (enduring vulnerability relative to a reference population)

Risk Status is a between-person comparison, not a within-person one. It asks:
compared with a specified reference population — people who contact this
service, people with a similar clinical profile, or the general population,
whichever is the appropriate comparator for the point being made — how elevated
is this person's enduring vulnerability? Name the comparison population you are
using; an unstated comparator is not a comparison.

Identify relatively enduring factors that establish that standing vulnerability
or resilience: previous suicidal behaviour, recurrent ideation, self-harm
history, enduring psychiatric difficulties, chronic pain/disability, recurrent
substance use, longstanding social adversity, established crisis/contact
patterns, previous help-seeking, enduring relationships and protections,
typical recovery pattern.

This is not a list of static risk factors, and it is not simply "this person's
own history" restated — that comparison belongs to Risk State, below. Where
information is insufficient to establish status against a reasonable
comparator, say so.

Guard against a specific circularity: a comparator built out of the case's own
features is not a comparator, even when phrased as one. "Compared with people
who contact this service with recurrent, chronic ideation and a multi-year
contact pattern, her presentation is..." is not a comparison to a reference
population — it is describing her against a group defined by her own
characteristics, which is not a validated group anyone has actually measured.
Without a real comparator (an epidemiological base rate, a clinically
recognised risk group, an organisational benchmark you actually have), say
plainly that status cannot be quantified against a validated comparator from
the information available, then state the enduring vulnerability factors
directly rather than dressing them up as a between-person comparison you don't
have data for.

### Step 3 — Risk State (change relative to this person's own baseline)

Risk State is a within-person comparison: the current presentation against this
specific person's own established or reasonably inferred baseline. This is
where "what is different now for them" belongs — do not do this comparison
under Risk Status. Look for change in: intensity, frequency, duration,
specificity, intent, planning, capability, access to means, hopelessness,
agitation, intoxication, isolation, engagement, behavioural control, psychotic
phenomena, contact pattern, capacity to use coping strategies, willingness to
accept help.

Avoid two automatic equations: distress does not automatically mean increased
suicide risk, and denial of suicidal intent does not automatically mean absence
of risk. State explicitly whether the information suggests no material change,
a temporary change, sustained/material deterioration, improvement, or
insufficient information to determine change.

### Step 4 — Resources (what is actually helping)

Differentiate between resources that merely exist and resources the person can
actually use — a sibling existing is not the same as a sibling being an
available protective resource; a safety plan existing is not the same as the
person being able to use it during acute distress. And differentiate both of
those from resources you simply have no information about — "not yet
confirmed as available and usable" is the correct description when the
material doesn't say, not "unavailable." Only call a resource unavailable if
that has actually been established (e.g. the person states they have no one
to call).

Consider internal resources (coping, problem-solving, emotional regulation,
hope, future orientation, reasons for living, capacity to delay action);
relational resources (trusted people, family, friends, peers, professionals);
service/system resources (treatment, medication, appointments, crisis services,
Lifeline, safeguarding arrangements); and environmental resources (supervision,
reduced access to lethal means, safe accommodation, practical containment). For
each important resource, ask: available, accessible, acceptable, and likely to
be used?

### Step 5 — Foreseeable Change

Identify plausible developments with a credible connection to risk — not
speculative catastrophising. Examples: intoxication later that night, a partner
leaving, a court outcome, contact from an abusive person, hospital discharge,
being alone after support leaves, access to medication, an anniversary, loss of
accommodation, non-attendance at a follow-up, further sleep deterioration,
resolution of an acute conflict.

Where relevant, name the foreseeable event, why it matters, what change would
indicate escalation, and what response should follow.

## Suicide enquiry integration

PORF does not replace direct suicide enquiry. Where suicide is relevant,
integrate available information on recent suicidal events, recent ideation,
historical suicidal behaviour, current/immediate intent, planning, access to
means, and actions already taken. Do not substitute a global risk label for
this information.

## Handling uncertainty

Represent uncertainty explicitly, using language like "unable to determine X
because…", "current information does not establish…", "this remains uncertain
because…", or "the apparent absence of X should be interpreted cautiously
because…".

Avoid "unassessable", "low risk" used solely because information is absent, "no
risk", and unsupported statements of future safety. Lack of information is
evidence of uncertainty, not evidence of elevated risk — its significance
depends on the surrounding formulation.

## Formulation synthesis

After working through Status, State, Resources and Foreseeable Change, produce
one integrated synthesis following this logic: **Because** (relevant
enduring/contextual factors) ... **Currently** (what has changed or not
changed) ... **However/protective factors** (what is reducing or containing
risk) ... **The main uncertainty is** (what cannot presently be established) ...
**Foreseeable change** (what could materially alter the formulation) ...
**Therefore** (the proportionate clinical response).

The "therefore" statement is mandatory. A formulation that does not influence
the response is incomplete.

Keep the "therefore" statement at the level of clinical response and required
level of escalation — not at the level of operational procedure. "This
warrants urgent emergency response" is a formulation-level conclusion.
Specifying how that response is executed (who to call, what sequence, what
scripts) is organisational procedure, and belongs to policy and the people
running the call, not to the formulation. Stopping at the right altitude also
keeps the reasoning honest: it's easier to smuggle unearned certainty into a
list of procedural steps than into a single statement of required response.

## Response categories

Consider, where the formulation supports it: continued Helpline management,
brief supportive intervention, safety planning, review of an existing safety
plan, facilitated linkage, Safety Contact, TL/Safety Huddle consultation,
enhanced assessment, CRG formulation, review of CMP/Access Agreement,
safeguarding pathway, or urgent emergency response.

When several concerning factors converge (e.g. acute loss, intoxication,
proximate means, unresolved intent), state the escalation as following from
the *decision under uncertainty* — routine management cannot safely contain
this level of unresolved concern — not as an automatic sum of factors ("access
+ intoxication + unanswered intent = emergency response"). The formulation
supports the decision; it shouldn't read as though the factor combination made
the decision by itself. Keep this distinction even when the response you land
on is the same either way — it's the difference between formulation-led
judgement and a scoring algorithm wearing formulation language.

Be similarly careful with intoxication's effects specifically: "likely
impairing capacity to delay action" states a clinical effect as though it were
established. Unless the degree of intoxication is actually known, prefer "may
reduce inhibition or capacity to delay action" — the direction is well
supported in general, the specific degree for this person tonight is not.

### Two kinds of escalation

Escalation is not only a response to elevated suicide risk. It is also the
right response to *persisting, unresolved clinical uncertainty* — cases where
repeated contacts each fail to establish enough to complete the formulation,
even though nothing in what was gathered points to acute risk. These are
different reasons to escalate and must not be collapsed into each other:

- **Risk-based escalation**: the formulation establishes a level of concern
  that routine management cannot safely contain.
- **Oversight/continuity escalation**: the Risk State cannot be established
  despite repeated opportunities, because contacts keep ending before enough
  information is gathered (disengagement, fragmentation across many short
  contacts, no collateral). Here the person's risk may remain genuinely
  unknown — the escalation is about coordinating across contacts, agreeing an
  engagement approach, and preventing what is really one unfolding
  presentation from being treated as several disconnected episodes with no
  continuity of judgement.

Do not slide from "elevated risk cannot be established" to "therefore
escalation is not warranted" — those are different claims. Persistent
inability to resolve the uncertainty, despite repeated genuine attempts, is
itself a reason for TL/Safety Huddle or equivalent oversight, on continuity
grounds, even while the Risk State itself stays honestly recorded as
unestablished.

Where a pattern of repeated, short, fragmented contact keeps ending before the
same enquiry can be completed, also consider whether the enquiry itself needs
to change. Repeated failure to obtain sufficient information using the current
interactional approach indicates a need to review and adapt that approach
rather than simply reproduce it — this is not a prediction that the same
approach will definitely fail again, only that it has not worked so far and
warrants review. The more useful clinical objective may be to understand what
is happening around the point of disengagement and adapt the approach to
sustain engagement past it, rather than instructing the next contact to simply
try harder at the same question.

Do not escalate merely because the person contacts frequently, distress is
intense, suicidal thoughts are chronic, the clinician feels anxious, or
information is incomplete — escalation must follow from the formulation, not
from any single one of these signals in isolation.

Hold this discipline through to the very last sentence you write. It's common
to reason correctly for most of a formulation and then let a rejected signal
back in at the "therefore" line — for instance, correctly establishing that
call frequency doesn't tell you the function of the contact, and then
recommending escalation "given the frequency" anyway. If an escalation
recommendation cites a signal the formulation itself said was inconclusive,
that's a contradiction — either the escalation needs a different, established
basis, or it isn't supported. The same applies to de-escalation triggers:
don't set "if contact stops" as a reason to revisit the formulation upward
unless stopping is actually linked to something risk-relevant — reduced
visibility is a reason to seek more information, not a risk indicator itself.

## Output format

Always structure the output like this:

```
PORF FORMULATION

Risk Status
[Concise narrative]

Risk State
[Concise comparison with baseline]

Resources
[Available and usable protective resources]

Foreseeable Change
[Specific plausible developments and relevant triggers for review]

Key uncertainties
[Only clinically material uncertainties]

Integrated formulation
[4-8 sentences synthesising the above]

Therefore
[One explicit statement describing the proportionate response and why]
```

## Quality checks

Before finalising, test the formulation against these questions and revise if
any answer is no:

1. Have facts been separated from inference?
2. Does Risk Status compare against a named reference population rather than
   restating the person's own history or listing risk factors?
3. Does Risk State explicitly compare today with this person's own baseline?
4. Has intensity of distress been distinguished from suicide risk?
5. Are protective resources actually available and usable?
6. Is foreseeable change plausible rather than generic?
7. Are clinically important uncertainties visible?
8. Has chronicity been prevented from obscuring meaningful change?
9. Does the formulation explain rather than merely describe?
10. Does the "therefore" response logically follow from the formulation, using
    only signals the formulation itself established rather than a rejected
    signal (e.g. bare frequency) sneaking back in at the last line?
11. Has any gap been filled with an invented comparator, an inferred capacity,
    or an "unavailable" where the honest answer was "not confirmed"?
12. Does the "therefore" statement stay at the level of clinical response
    rather than prescribing operational/procedural detail?

## Reasoning shortcuts to avoid

These conclusions look plausible but skip the comparative reasoning the
formulation depends on — never let one fact stand in for the whole picture:

- "Chronic suicidal thoughts therefore no increased concern."
- "Frequent calls therefore high risk."
- "Denies intent therefore low risk."
- "Has protective factors therefore safe."
- "Unable to complete assessment therefore emergency response."
- "Previous similar presentations resolved therefore this one will."
- Inferring motivation or function from contact frequency alone.
- "No baseline available therefore this represents a deterioration" — with no
  baseline, you can describe acute indicators, not a change from an unknown
  starting point.
- "No resources confirmed therefore no resources exist."
- "No prior attempts therefore an established capacity to manage the
  ideation" — absence of behaviour is not evidence of a coping mechanism.
- "Contact has stopped therefore risk has increased" — cessation of contact
  is a loss of visibility, not a risk signal in itself.

## Style

Write in clinical, concise, formulation-led prose. Prefer explanation over
labelling:

- Prefer "Suicidal thoughts appear consistent with her established baseline;
  however, tonight there is new access to medication and reduced confidence in
  her ability to delay acting." over "Risk is high because she is suicidal."
- Prefer "The function of the repeated contacts cannot be established from call
  frequency alone." over "She is reassurance-seeking."
- Prefer "The current information does not establish whether intent has
  changed." over "Patient is unassessable."

## Boundary

This skill provides structured clinical reasoning. It does not independently
diagnose, determine capacity, replace organisational emergency procedures,
replace safeguarding procedures, make treatment decisions outside available
information, infer motive from behaviour, or predict suicide. Where
organisational policy specifies a mandatory action, policy takes precedence
over this skill.
